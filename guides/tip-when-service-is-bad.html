What to Do When Service Is Bad

Bad service is frustrating, but a very small tip or a polite word with the manager usually communicates more than leaving nothing. This guide explains what to do when service is bad in plain language, shows the maths with a real worked example, and points out the mistakes people make most often. You can follow along with the tip calculator on this site, and by the end you will be able to check any result by hand and use the calculator with confidence.

How it works

There are two parts to what to do when service is bad: getting the inputs right and reading the output honestly. The inputs are your responsibility, and they deserve a minute of care, because a calculator will happily produce a precise-looking answer from a careless guess. The output is the calculator's job, and it is worth reading the whole output, not just the biggest number. The extra figures, such as the total interest or the amount saved, often tell you more than the headline result.

A good way to build confidence is to try the calculation twice with slightly different numbers and watch how the answer moves. If a small change in one input causes a huge change in the answer, that input deserves extra care. If the answer barely moves, you can relax about it. This kind of sensitivity check takes a minute and turns a calculator from a black box into a tool you actually understand.

Worked example

Take a worked example. A bill of 55 with a tip of 1 percent gives a tip of 5.5, because 55 times 1 divided by one hundred is 5.5. The total to pay is 60.5. If 1 person pays, each person pays 60.5, which includes a tip share of 5.5. Rounding the tip up to the next whole number would make the tip 6 and the total 61, a difference of only 0.5, which is why rounding up is a painless way to be a little more generous without any extra maths.

Common mistakes

Watch out for three traps. First, a guessed input is not a measured one, and the answer is only as honest as the guess. Second, a single result is a snapshot, not a trend, so one reading should never drive a big decision on its own. Third, averages hide variation, so a result that looks fine on average may still be wrong for your particular case.

  • Entering numbers in the wrong units or the wrong order.
  • Rounding in the middle of the calculation instead of at the end.
  • Trusting the first result without a rough check against common sense.

Practical tips

Use the calculator as a starting point, not a final verdict. Try the optimistic and the pessimistic version of your numbers to see the range of possible answers, and plan around the worse one if the decision matters. If the result will affect money or health, compare it with a second source or ask a professional, because a free online tool is a guide, not advice.

Tip: change one input at a time and watch how the answer moves. That shows you which number matters most and where extra care pays off.

What to do when service is bad

Bad service is frustrating, and the tip is the one place where a customer can respond directly. The fairest approach is to reduce the tip rather than remove it entirely, unless the problem was serious and clearly the server's fault. A small tip, together with a calm word about what went wrong, tells the server more than silence or an empty table ever could.

It helps to separate the server from the kitchen and the restaurant. A long wait caused by a busy kitchen is not the server's fault, and neither is a dish that was cooked badly, so the tip should reflect how the server handled the problem rather than the problem itself.

Whatever you decide, stay polite about it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I trust a single result?

A single result is a snapshot. It is useful, but big decisions deserve more than one reading: try different inputs, check the trend over time, and compare with a second source when the stakes are high.

What should I do if the answer looks wrong?

First re-read every input and check the units. Then try a rough estimate by hand. If the calculator still disagrees with your estimate, the input is almost always the problem, so correct it and calculate again.

No. The calculations happen in your browser and your numbers are not sent to us or stored on any server. Closing the page removes them.
